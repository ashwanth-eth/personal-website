Civic Pulse

December 2025

I've always thought scraping the web as a source of data was such an interesting concept. I learned it with Beautiful Soup during my data science master's program at UVA, which was a pretty arduous process. On my first case at Bain, I scraped a product review site to run a sentiment analysis for a cybersecurity diligence (story for another time). Since then I've burned through free credits on Octoparse and Firecrawl without ever feeling it was worth paying.

This fall I took CS 1060 at Harvard, where the goal is to build a piece of software you could theoretically sell. Vibe coding is kind of a thing, and Cursor has a free student plan, so I hammered those models to see whether scraping had gotten any easier.

The question was what to scrape. I wanted information on the web that has never been useful because nobody has compiled it all together, and local governments came to mind pretty quickly. I was on my city's teen council back in the day, and I remember sitting in city council meetings watching somebody take all these notes. Who ever sees those notes? I grew up in Kansas, so I started there. Some friends going through public markets recruiting said it could be worth a look.

A funny thing about CS 1060 is that it mixes undergrad and master's students from across Harvard. My team was two seniors from Harvard College, a master's student in data science, and me, a second-year MBA at HBS. None of us had done a full software project with a team, and we almost immediately started hitting merge conflicts and ripping our hair out. My job was the scraping, which Cursor made fast.

I also made 15 to 20 calls to the people who take notes at city council meetings. It was one of the first times I did true customer discovery. My first call was to the person who takes notes for the Lenexa City Council, and it got away from me immediately. I mentioned something about AI pulling information from the minutes, and she started asking me about water usage and data centers. I just wanted to know why they take notes, why they post them on their website, and who reads them. After that, I asked the simple questions first. The people who picked up were often extremely bored, so they were willing to talk.

Once the data came in, we didn't find much. One local government had a budget to buy a tank, which raised questions about oversight, but nobody was looking for this data. In hindsight, I only talked to the people who publish the minutes, never to anyone who would use them. We also threw away a couple of UI mockups along the way, and as The Mythical Man-Month would suggest, we probably should have thrown away more.

We've shut it down now, but I learned a ton this semester. Mostly, that you can just pick up the phone and call people.
