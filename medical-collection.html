Can Claude Clear a Collection I Never Owed?

An unexpected letter from collections sent my credit score plummeting while I was looking for a new apartment.

November 2026

I've been on a side quest over the last five years of tearing my ACLs and menisci. Between 2019 and 2024, I tore up my left knee once and my right knee twice. The recovery each time has been absolutely brutal. But easily, and this is not even close, the worst part has been dealing with the nightmare that is insurance companies.

Every surgery came with a mountain of paperwork. My MRI might happen at one facility and an ER visit at another, sometimes within the same hospital network, and each one had its own billing system with its own record and claim numbers. So I'd get mail from all of these places and have to match strings of letters and numbers to the right case. I have spent an astounding number of hours on the phone. When you get a bill that mistakenly says you owe $20K and have to pay ASAP or get sent to collections, it takes a toll on you mentally. I was grateful to have folks in my life who knew the system and told me not to worry.

Mistakes like that happen a lot. In a 2022 KFF survey, 43% of US adults said they'd gotten a medical or dental bill they thought had an error. Nobody tracks how many people just pay those bills, but in a Michigan survey, about 1 in 5 people who got an unexpected medical bill paid it without questioning it.

Since 2024, I have been extremely careful not to put my knees in precarious situations (no more sports with balls!). So imagine my surprise when, early this summer, my credit score plummeted. Alerts from the credit bureaus said a new collection account had shown up on my report. Then I found the letter, which I'd missed because we'd been traveling. A collection agency was coming after me for a hospital bill from 2019 that I had never seen. My wife and I were just starting to look for apartments in Atlanta, and I knew every landlord would run my credit.

The letter said I owed a little over $600, and on the back, it offered a 35% discount if I paid within 35 days. I didn't call anyone this time. I didn't even know what to ask. In a state of panic, I took a picture of the letter and uploaded it to Claude. The first thing Claude pointed out was a paragraph I hadn't even noticed, in small type and all caps, right under the amount I supposedly owed:

From the collection letter

This debt may be too old for you to be sued on it in court. If it is too old, you cannot be required to pay it through a lawsuit. Take note: you can renew the debt and the statute of limitations for the filing of a lawsuit against you if you do any of the following: make any payment on the debt … While this debt may not be enforceable through a lawsuit, it may still affect your ability to obtain credit or affect your credit score or rating.

Claude explained that paying the discount could have restarted the clock on a debt that was too old to sue me over. That's when I thought, maybe I should upload all of my letters and documentation and let Claude hold my hand through the whole process. So I did, explained the situation, and asked for a plan of attack:

  1. Don't pay, don't call the collector, and don't put anything in writing that says the debt is mine.
  2. Call the hospital and ask whether the bill exists at all. Claude wrote me a script for the call.
  3. Pull my reports from all three credit bureaus and dispute the collection with each one.
  4. Send the collector a certified letter at both of its mailing addresses, asking it to prove I owed anything.
  5. File complaints with the CFPB and the Massachusetts attorney general.
  6. Keep my credit frozen, turn on alerts, and check every month that the collection never comes back.

What Claude told me to say on the call

Say: "I'm not paying today and I'm not acknowledging the debt. I just need the records."

Don't say: "Yes, that's right." Don't say: "I can pay something."

The hospital told me (twice) that it had no record of the bill. One of the credit bureaus deleted the collection in under a minute, which tells you how hard it looked, and the other two followed. After my CFPB complaint, the collector paused everything and admitted it didn't have an itemized bill. That mattered, because an itemized bill is how a collector proves what you were charged for and that the charge is yours. Without one, it had nothing to back up the claim. About three weeks after I filed the disputes, my score had bounced back, we'd passed the apartment credit check, and I started getting messages like this one: "On your previous report you had 1 collection, now you have 0."

If I had tried to solve this on my own, it would have taken me days, plus all the stress. Claude drafted every letter and dispute and put every deadline on my calendar. It remembered every case number and reference number, so I never went digging for them. And it kept a running record of what we had and hadn't done, so every time I came back, we picked up where we left off.

My hope is that more people get access to these tools and learn how to use them on burdensome problems like this one. Plenty of people don't have someone who knows the system to call, and they're one PAY NOW letter away from paying a bill they don't owe.
